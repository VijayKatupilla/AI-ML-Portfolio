Artifact 03

Neural Networks, Explained Simply

Instructional Design Strategic AI Prompting Technical Accuracy Review
Introduction

This artifact is a discussion-assignment explanation of neural networks, built by deliberately applying communication-for-learning strategies while working with a generative AI, rather than accepting its first draft.

Objective

Built for a discussion assignment on communication in change leadership: apply deliberate communication-for-learning strategies — chunking, progressive disclosure, storytelling — while working with a generative AI to produce a genuinely simple explanation of neural networks, rather than accepting the AI's first draft.

Process

Studied the workshop's communication and information-presentation strategies, then prompted an AI to build a single sustained analogy — a relay race, where each runner's performance depends on the handoff from the runner before them — instead of several unrelated comparisons. Caught and corrected a real accuracy gap in an earlier draft: an independent-experts analogy made backpropagation sound like each layer corrects itself, when it actually corrects backward through the network in sequence, each layer's fix dependent on the one after it. Revised until the analogy held up to that follow-up question.

Tools and Tech Used

ChatGPT for creating the image, Claude (Anthropic) for drafting and iterating the explanation; course lesson material and IBM's neural network reference articles, used to verify the analogy's technical accuracy before publishing.

The Explanation — A Relay Race

Four runners on a team, each taking the baton from the one before and running their leg. How well each runner does depends on their own speed and how clean the handoff was — a bad handoff slows even a fast runner. That combination is a neuron: it takes what it's handed, weighs it against its own ability, and passes something forward. Stack four legs like that and you've got layers, with the anchor runner's finish time as the final result.

Here's what makes it learning: the team loses, and the coach works backward from the finish line — was the anchor's slow finish their own pace, or a bad handoff from runner 3? Once she knows that, she can tell runner 3 exactly how much to improve. But to know if runner 3 ran their own leg well, she has to check whether they got a bad handoff from runner 2. The correction moves backward down the chain, and each runner's fix depends on the fix already worked out for the runner after them. That's backpropagation: the network traces the final error backward layer by layer, each correction calculated using the correction already figured out for the layer after it.

Artifact 04

Data Challenges in Machine Learning

Data Preprocessing Applied Data Science Technical Decision-Making
Introduction

This artifact is a write-up of three real-world data challenge scenarios I worked through with an AI coach: missing data, class imbalance, and data drift.

Objective

Applied core data-quality, class-imbalance, and data-drift concepts to realistic ML scenarios through an AI-coached activity, demonstrating practical judgment in choosing between competing data engineering techniques rather than reciting definitions.

Process

Worked through three scenario-based problems with an AI coach — missing values in a customer dataset, severe class imbalance in a fraud detection dataset, and a housing-price model degrading in production months after deployment — reasoning through the tradeoffs of each available technique against the specifics of the scenario rather than defaulting to the first plausible answer.

Tools and Tech Used

AI-coached scenario activity (SchoolAI), ChatGPT for infographic design.

Scenario 3 — Data Drift

A housing-price model may lose accuracy over time even when its code has not changed because the incoming data or the relationship between the input features and housing prices has shifted. Changes in the input distribution are commonly described as feature or data drift, while changes in the relationship between the inputs and the target can be considered concept drift. The appropriate response is ongoing monitoring rather than a one-time fix. Teams can monitor feature distributions and prediction distributions for early warning signals, and compare predictions with newly available ground-truth outcomes to confirm whether model performance has actually degraded.

Personal Framework for AI/ML Leadership

Self-Reflection Strategic Planning Leadership Synthesis
Introduction

This artifact brings together my learning from the course into a personal framework for how I want to approach AI/ML leadership. It connects my original leadership and AI/ML integration self-assessments with what I learned about communication, iterative leadership, responsible AI, stakeholder alignment, adaptability, and continuous professional development.

Objective

The objective was to define the type of AI/ML leader I want to become and translate that vision into a practical framework containing a mission statement, core values, measurable objectives, action plans, and a process for evaluation and adaptation.

Process

Reviewed my original Change Management Skills Self-Assessment and AI/ML Integration Leadership Skills Self-Assessment, identified strengths and areas for improvement, and reflected on major course concepts including iterative leadership, communication during change, stakeholder alignment, ethical AI, human bias, responsible AI/ML integration, feedback loops, and continuous learning. I then translated those reflections into a mission statement, core values, measurable objectives, short-term and long-term action plans, and a recurring evaluation process.

Tools and Tech Used

Change Management Skills Self-Assessment, AI/ML Integration Leadership Skills Self-Assessment, AIML-500 course materials, reflection and self-evaluation, and ChatGPT for organizing and refining the framework based on my own assessment results and course reflections.

Mission Statement

My mission is to lead AI/ML initiatives that connect technical innovation with responsible decision-making while creating teams that communicate openly, learn through iteration, and adapt as technology, evidence, and organizational needs change.

Core Values
  • Responsibility and Ethical Stewardship — AI/ML leadership should consider both technical performance and the consequences of how systems are used. Responsibility should be shared across technical and organizational teams.
  • Transparency — Leaders should communicate clearly about AI capabilities, limitations, uncertainty, and decision-making.
  • Continuous and Iterative Learning — Plans should evolve based on evidence, feedback, changing technology, and organizational needs.
  • Collaboration and Shared Accountability — AI/ML integration requires engineers, data scientists, business leaders, users, and other stakeholders to contribute different perspectives.
Key Objectives
  • Strengthen End-to-End AI/ML Change Leadership — Improve my ability to lead AI/ML initiatives from planning through implementation and evaluation.
  • Make Responsible AI Part of Development — Include ethical considerations such as bias, fairness, transparency, limitations, and user impact during development rather than treating them as an afterthought.
  • Build Stronger Feedback and Measurement Practices — Define measurable success indicators and feedback mechanisms for significant AI/ML initiatives.
  • Develop People Alongside Technology — Improve my ability to prepare and support people through AI/ML change through knowledge sharing, training, and communication.
Action Plans

Short term, I will continue developing my technical AI/ML knowledge while connecting that knowledge to business impact, ethics, and change leadership. I will seek feedback after meaningful leadership or collaborative experiences and use a simple pre-implementation checklist covering objectives, stakeholders, risks, ethical considerations, success measures, and feedback.

Long term, I will continue my technical education, seek mentorship from experienced AI/ML leaders, take on more cross-functional responsibilities, and create structured knowledge-sharing opportunities that help others understand and adapt to AI/ML technologies.

Evaluation and Adaptation

I will treat this framework as a living document. Each month, I will reflect on one leadership situation. After major AI/ML projects or milestones, I will conduct a retrospective and seek stakeholder feedback. Quarterly, I will review progress against my objectives. Every six months, I will repeat selected parts of my original self-assessments and compare the results with real examples from my work. Annually, I will review and revise the entire framework based on new responsibilities, feedback, and development priorities.
